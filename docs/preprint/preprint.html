Key points

  • "Sleeping beauties" are papers ignored for years and then suddenly heavily cited. So far they have only been identified after they woke up.
  • We built and preregistered an open, $0 engine (keyword search + meaning search + re-reading by an open-weights language model) and a protocol to test whether it can find them before they wake up.
  • In the exploratory run (21 development sleeping beauties), the engine put the hidden paper in its top 10 of 1,000 candidates for 8 of 21 (38%), against 1% by chance. Plain hybrid search did the same (8 of 21), so the AI re-reading did not add top-10 hits on this sample.
  • On 4 practice antibiotic-resistance problems, three blind AI judges rated the engine's forgotten pre-1990 picks higher than random controls (mean 3.10 vs 2.18 and 1.45 on a 1-5 scale).
  • The confirmatory test (78 locked papers, 20 preregistered problems) has not been run. All hypotheses, thresholds, prompts and code were frozen on 2026-11-27 (bundle SHA-256 508bdc28…9a18).

Abstract

Background. Ke et al. (2015) showed that delayed-recognition papers ("sleeping beauties") are far more common than once thought, but every study so far finds them retrospectively, from citation curves. No system has been tested on whether it could have found them early.

Methods. From a seeded sample of 6,000 OpenAlex articles (1900-1985, at least 100 citations), we computed the Ke et al. beauty coefficient B and selected the top 100 sleeping beauties (slept at least 10 years, woke 1950-2018, B at least 50). A fixed SHA-256 rule split them into 21 development and 79 confirmatory items. For each item, an open-weights model summarised the open problems of the waking field from 20 abstracts published the year before the awakening; five methods then ranked the true paper among 999 same-field decoys. A forward test asks the engine to surface forgotten (25 or fewer citations) pre-1990 papers for open problems in antibiotic resistance, rated blind by three AI judges from a different model family. Every AI model had to pass a known-answer gate first.

Exploratory results (development data only). Engine hit@10 was 8/21 (38%; Wilson 95% CI 21-59%; chance 1%), equal to hybrid search (8/21; exact McNemar p = 0.75). Mean reciprocal rank was 0.254 for the engine and 0.197 for hybrid search. The engine model "recognised" 14/21 papers, and all 8 hits were among them, but hybrid search showed the same split (7 of its 8 hits), so recognised papers may simply be easier. On 4 practice problems, engine picks scored 3.10 vs 2.18 (same-pool random) and 1.45 (global random); judge agreement on applicability was alpha = 0.559. All 4 models passed the gate (AUC 0.66-0.87); 11/11 self-checks passed; total cost was $0.

Status. These are exploratory numbers. The confirmatory study is preregistered on OSF and frozen; its results will be reported whatever they show.

1. Why this matters

Science has many examples of work that waited decades for its moment. A tool that can match an old, overlooked method to a modern open problem would turn the unread literature into a resource. Until recently this could not be attempted on purpose: it requires reading old papers against every modern problem. Language models can now read abstracts at scale and judge whether an old method fits a new need. The open question is whether they actually do this, or only recognise famous papers they saw in training. This study is designed to tell those two apart.

2.2 The back-test: could the engine have found it early?

For each sleeping beauty that woke in year Y, the "waking field" is the most common OpenAlex topic among papers citing it in years Y to Y+3. From that field we take up to 20 abstracts published in Y-1 that do not cite the sleeping beauty, and the engine model writes the field's open problems from them alone, naming no solutions. The sleeping beauty is then hidden among 999 decoys (same field, published within 3 years of it) and ranked by five methods on the same candidate set:

MethodWhat it does
RandomSeeded random order (the luck baseline: 10 in 1,000 = 1%)
Keyword (BM25)Word-overlap search
Meaning (dense)Embedding similarity, BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5, run locally
Hybrid searchReciprocal-rank fusion of keyword and meaning search (k = 60)
Wake-up EngineHybrid search, then the top 50 re-read and scored 0-10 by the engine model (qwen/qwen3.8-27b)

A hit is the true sleeping beauty ranked in the top 10 of 1,000. Every paper the models see is anonymised (author names, eponyms, years, DOIs, URLs, IDs and "et al." removed; venue never shown).

2.3 The forward test: what could it wake today?

For each open problem, the engine searches OpenAlex for papers published before 1990 with 25 or fewer citations ("forgotten"), re-ranks them, and its top 5 are mixed with 5 random papers from the same search pool and 5 random forgotten pre-1990 papers. Three judges, none from the engine's model family (openai/gpt-oss-120b, openai/gpt-oss-20b, allam-2-7b), rate each paper alone and blind on applicability and novelty (1-5).

2.4 Safeguards built in

  • Lock: a SHA-256 rule on the OpenAlex ID decides development vs confirmatory; the exploratory run only touched development items and 4 practice problems.
  • Known-answer gate: each model must rank real sleeping beauties above decoys (AUC above 0.5, permutation p below 0.05) and must not give over 95% identical scores.
  • Recognition probe and disguised text: tests whether the model is remembering a famous paper rather than reasoning.
  • Leak scan and 11 self-checks: every prompt is scanned for clues; key numbers are recomputed a second way.
  • Fingerprints: every prompt, reply and output file carries a SHA-256 hash; the 28 study files are frozen in one bundle hash.

The honest catch: recognition or reasoning?

The engine model recognised 14 of 21 development papers (it also falsely "recognised" 21% of decoys). All 8 engine hits were recognised; 0 of 7 unrecognised papers were hit. But hybrid search, which has no memory, shows the same split (7 of its 8 hits were recognised papers), and rewriting the texts in other words did not hurt the engine (mean rank 4.67 vs 4.44, Wilcoxon p = 0.51). So recognised papers may simply be easier to match. The confirmatory study tests this directly with preregistered secondary test S1 (engine hits on unrecognised papers only).

The confirmatory back-test uses the 78 remaining locked sleeping beauties (at least 50 usable required). With n = 50 the minimum detectable hit rate at 80% power is 8.4%; with n = 80 it is 5.3%. The forward test covers 20 preregistered antibiotic-resistance problems (300 papers, 900 ratings). Alpha is 0.05, one-sided. A non-significant result will be read against the minimum detectable effect and called inconclusive where underpowered.

5. Transparency: one disclosed incident

On 2026-11-27, after the first freeze and before registration, a faulty test started the confirmatory command twice. Both starts were stopped after 120 seconds while still on confirmatory item 1 of 79 (OpenAlex W1993374508): 1 need summary and re-rank scores for 29 decoys were requested. The sleeping beauty itself was not scored, no result was computed, and no reply was read. The author excluded that item (decision D20), the test was fixed, both partial runs are kept unchanged as evidence, and the study was re-frozen at 2026-11-27T15:13:50Z.

6. Limitations

  • Hindsight: today's models were trained on text written after these papers woke up, so a positive back-test is an upper bound.
  • Small exploratory sample: 21 papers and 4 practice problems; wide confidence intervals.
  • Judges are AI, not experts: forward-test hits are leads, not confirmed rediscoveries. Two of three judges share the gpt-oss family, which may inflate agreement.
  • Data coverage: OpenAlex has sparse abstracts for old papers; patents and scanned archives are not included in version 0.2.0.
  • Free endpoints change: a retired model would be a reported deviation.
